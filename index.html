EXHIBIT 01 · THE TUG OF WAR

POWER UP. SMOKE DOWN?

Across the region, electricity generation climbed like a carnival strongman's bell, hit after hit, year after year. Meanwhile the average tonne of greenhouse gas per person crept downward. Two lines, one arena. But is it really the win it looks like? Step closer: the power line is almost suspiciously straight, the kind of climb you'd expect from steady grid build out rather than any single dramatic event. The emissions line is messier, it wobbles, it has a personality, and as you'll see in Exhibit 03, one very small nation is quietly doing most of the work to pull it down.

EXHIBIT 06½ · UNDER THE HOOD

WHAT IS K-MEANS, AND WHY USE IT HERE?

K-means is an unsupervised clustering algorithm, it groups data points into k clusters based purely on similarity, with no ready made "answer" to learn from. Each point joins the cluster whose center it's closest to; each center then moves to sit at the average of its group; the two steps repeat until nothing changes.

BEFORE

One ungrouped scatter of 17 islands' emission trend shapes, no structure visible yet.

→ K-MEANS, k=3 →
AFTER

Three groups fall out: one Palau like outlier tier, a mid pack tier, and a low/flat tier.

In this report, k-means runs on each island's standardized 55-year GHG per capita trajectory shape (not raw levels) with k=3. Standardizing first matters: it stops a large economy's sheer scale from dominating the grouping, so the algorithm groups countries by how their emissions trend behaves, flat, rising, or wildly volatile, rather than by size. Without that step, the clustering would just rediscover "who's biggest," which the raw value bar chart above already shows for free; standardizing forces it to find something genuinely new: shape, not scale. That's what separates Palau (a statistical population of one, per Exhibit 03) from the other 16 islands, and it's the justification for not folding every country into a single regional average line. It's also why New Caledonia, large in absolute terms but steady in trajectory, lands in a different bin than its raw GHG total alone would suggest, and why the mid pack cluster of fifteen islands can be described with one trend line without materially misrepresenting any single member of it.

See Exhibit 06 above for the real result on actual island data, and Exhibit 06⅞ below for how we checked the choice of k=3 wasn't arbitrary (silhouette score).

📏 EXHIBIT 06⅞A · R², SHARE OF VARIANCE EXPLAINED

R² measures how much of the up and down movement in the data the straight line actually accounts for, on a 0 to 1 scale. Power generation scores 0.915, near perfect. GHG per capita scores 0.666, good but noisier, consistent with a messier underlying process driven by one country's story more than a smooth regional trend.

See also: "Fit Quality, Head to Head" in the Results Hall above.

📐 EXHIBIT 06⅞B · RMSE / MAE, THE TYPICAL SIZE OF A MISS

Root-mean-square error and mean-absolute error report the typical miss size in the original units, GWh for power, tonnes per capita for emissions. That makes them easier to sanity check at a glance than R² alone, since R² is unitless and abstract while RMSE/MAE tell you directly "how many gigawatt hours off was this line, on a normal year?"

See also: the "Residuals" panel in the Results Hall's regression fit exhibit.

🔁 EXHIBIT 06⅞C · LEAVE-ONE-OUT CROSS-VALIDATION

With roughly 17 to 24 points per series, we refit the line while leaving one year out at a time, then checked how well the line predicted that missing year. The held out prediction error stayed close to the in-sample error, meaning the trend isn't just memorizing a handful of points, it's picking up a real pattern that generalizes to years it never saw.

A natural supporting view: predicted versus actual for each held out year, hugging the diagonal.

🧪 EXHIBIT 06⅞D · RESIDUAL DIAGNOSTICS

Residuals, the gap between the actual value and what the line predicted, were checked for pattern. They should look like scattered static, not a curve or a wave. A curved residual plot would be a red flag that a straight line is the wrong shape for the underlying process, no matter how high the R².

See also: the residual panel already shown in the Results Hall above.

🎯 EXHIBIT 06⅞E · SILHOUETTE SCORE

For the k-means clustering in Exhibit 06, silhouette score compares how close each island sits to its own assigned cluster versus the next nearest cluster. A higher score means the groupings are well separated rather than arbitrary. It's what let us settle on k=3 (Palau alone, New Caledonia alone, and the other fifteen islands together) instead of guessing at a cluster count.

A natural supporting view: silhouette score at k=2, k=3, k=4, showing k=3 as the peak.

⚖️ EXHIBIT 06⅞F · PEARSON CORRELATION + P-VALUE

For the power growth versus emissions growth question in Exhibit 04, Pearson's r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship (r = 0.18, weak). The p-value (0.53) is what lets us say "not statistically significant" with some rigor, rather than just eyeballing a weak looking scatter and guessing. A p-value that high means a relationship this weak could easily show up by chance alone, even if no real link exists.

See also: the scatter plot in Exhibit 04, with the (non-)fit line drawn through it.

None of this makes the forecasts precise, with only 17 to 55 annual points per series, these are directional reads, not engineering grade predictions. The metrics exist so you can judge how much to trust the line, not just look at it and guess.
